Corns and Calluses: A Complete Guide to Removal and Prevention

An educational guide from the foot care team at Imperial Feet

If you have ever felt a tender, hardened patch of skin on your foot that makes every step uncomfortable, you have likely dealt with a corn or a callus. These thickened areas of skin are incredibly common. Millions of people develop them at some point, often without realizing that their footwear or daily habits are the reason. The good news is that corns and calluses are not dangerous in most healthy people, and with the right approach they can be managed safely at home and prevented from coming back. This guide explains what corns and calluses are, why they form, the different types, how to remove them safely, what you should never do, how to prevent them, and when it is time to visit a podiatrist.

What Are Corns and Calluses?

Corns and calluses are areas of thickened, hardened skin that develop as a protective response. When a specific spot on your foot is repeatedly subjected to friction or pressure, your skin defends itself by producing extra layers of a tough protein called keratin. Over time, those layers build up into a dense patch that feels rough or waxy. Think of it as your body building its own padding against a constant irritation.

The main difference between the two lies in size, shape, and location. A corn is usually small, round, and has a dense, hard center that presses inward toward the bone. Corns most often appear on the tops and sides of the toes, and sometimes between the toes where bones rub together. A callus, by contrast, tends to be broader, flatter, and less defined at the edges. Calluses typically form on the soles of the feet, especially under the heel and the ball of the foot, where body weight creates sustained pressure.

Neither corns nor calluses are caused by a virus or bacteria, and they are not contagious. They are purely mechanical: remove the source of the friction or pressure, and the thickened skin will usually stop building up. That said, ignoring them can make walking painful and, in people with certain medical conditions, can lead to complications that deserve professional attention.

What Causes Corns and Calluses?

Corns and calluses always have a trigger. Understanding the trigger is the first step toward getting rid of them for good.

Friction and Pressure

Repetitive rubbing is the single most common cause. A shoe that slides slightly with each step, a seam that presses against a toe, or a foot that shifts inside a loose sandal can all create friction. Pressure is the other half of the equation. When body weight concentrates on a small area, such as the ball of the foot in high heels or the heel in flat shoes with no cushioning, the skin thickens to cope.

Ill-Fitting Shoes

Shoes that are too tight squeeze the toes together, causing the bones of the toes to rub against each other and against the shoe. Shoes that are too loose let the foot slide, which produces friction. High-heeled shoes deserve special mention because they push the foot forward, crowding the toes and concentrating pressure on the forefoot. Wearing shoes without socks increases friction further, since bare skin rubs directly against the shoe material.

Foot Shape and Gait

Some people are more prone to corns and calluses because of the natural shape of their feet. Bunions, hammertoes, and bony prominences change how pressure is distributed across the foot, creating hot spots where skin thickens. The way you walk also matters. If you overpronate or supinate, certain areas of the sole take more load than they should, and calluses form there as a response.

Activities and Lifestyle

People who spend long hours standing, walking on hard surfaces, running, or doing manual labor in stiff boots tend to develop calluses more often. Musicians, athletes, and workers who use their hands repetitively develop calluses on their palms for the same reason: the skin adapts to repeated mechanical stress.

The Different Types

Not all thickened skin is the same. Identifying which type you have helps you choose the right treatment.

Hard Corns

Hard corns are the most familiar type. They are small, round, and dense, with a hard central core surrounded by inflamed or thickened skin. They usually develop on the tops of the toes or on the outer edge of the little toe, in spots that press against a shoe. Because the hard center presses inward, hard corns can be quite painful when pressed.

Soft Corns

Soft corns form between the toes, where the skin stays moist from perspiration. They look whitish or gray and feel rubbery rather than hard. The fourth and fifth toes are the classic location. Soft corns can be especially uncomfortable because the damp environment between the toes can make the skin macerated, and an infection can develop if the skin cracks.

Seed Corns

Seed corns are a distinct group of tiny, well-defined corns that appear on the sole of the foot. They often show up in clusters and can be very tender when you bear weight on them. Seed corns are sometimes mistaken for plantar warts, but unlike warts they are not caused by a virus and do not have black pinpoint dots of clotted blood vessels in the center.

Calluses

Calluses are larger and more diffuse than corns. They rarely hurt on their own, though very thick calluses can crack, especially on the heels, and cracked calluses can be painful and prone to infection. Most calluses on the feet form under the metatarsal heads, the ball of the foot, or around the heel. A callus is essentially the skin doing its job, but when it becomes too thick it needs attention.

Safe At-Home Removal

For otherwise healthy people, corns and calluses can often be reduced gradually at home with gentle, consistent care. The key word is gradual. Thickened skin built up over months, and it should be reduced over weeks, not scraped away in a single aggressive session.

Soaking

Soaking the foot in warm water for ten to fifteen minutes softens the thickened skin and makes it much easier to work with. Warm water alone is enough, although some people add a mild soap. After soaking, pat the feet dry with a clean towel. Never soak your feet if you have diabetes, poor circulation, or any open cracks or sores, because soaking can increase the risk of infection in those situations.

Pumice Stone or Foot File

After soaking, gently rub the thickened area with a pumice stone or a foot file, using light pressure and smooth, circular motions. The goal is to remove thin layers of dead skin a little at a time. Rinse the stone after use and let it dry to keep it hygienic. Stop immediately if the area becomes painful, red, or starts to bleed. Doing this two or three times a week, combined with daily moisturizing, can reduce a callus significantly over several weeks.

Salicylic Acid Products

Over-the-counter products containing salicylic acid are available as liquids, gels, pads, or plasters. Salicylic acid is a keratolytic, which means it softens and loosens the thickened keratin so it can be worn away. These products can be effective for persistent corns, but they must be used carefully. Apply them only to the thickened area, protect the surrounding healthy skin, and follow the product instructions exactly. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or fragile skin should not use salicylic acid corn removers without medical advice, because the acid can damage healthy skin and create wounds that heal slowly.

Moisturizing

Keeping the skin supple helps prevent cracks and slows the buildup of new thick skin. Apply a thick foot cream or urea-based moisturizer after bathing and before bed. Regular moisturizing will not remove an existing corn on its own, but it keeps the surrounding skin flexible and makes mechanical reduction easier and safer.

Important: If you have diabetes, peripheral artery disease, or neuropathy, skip home removal entirely and see a podiatrist. Reduced sensation and poor circulation turn minor skin damage into a serious risk.

What NOT to Do

Some popular shortcuts for dealing with corns and calluses are unsafe and can cause real harm.

  • Do not cut or shave a corn or callus yourself. Using a razor blade, scalpel, knife, or scissors on thickened skin risks cutting too deep, causing bleeding, and introducing infection. This is one of the most common causes of foot infections that start as a simple corn.
  • Do not use medicated corn plasters if you have diabetes. Corn plasters contain salicylic acid, which can burn the surrounding healthy skin. In a person with diabetes or poor circulation, that chemical burn can become an ulcer that is slow to heal and vulnerable to serious infection.
  • Do not pick at soft corns between the toes. The moist skin there tears easily, and open skin between the toes is an invitation for fungal or bacterial infection.
  • Do not expect a one-time fix. Aggressively filing or peeling a callus in a single session often leaves the skin raw and sore, and the thickened skin usually returns quickly because the underlying pressure has not changed.
  • Do not ignore signs of infection. Increasing redness, swelling, warmth, pus, or red streaks spreading from a corn or callus are warning signs. Seek medical care promptly rather than continuing home treatment.

Prevention: Stop Them Coming Back

Removal without prevention is a cycle. The following habits address the root causes so corns and calluses stay away.

Wear Properly Fitting Footwear

Shoes should have enough room for your toes to wiggle without sliding around inside. Shop for shoes later in the day, when feet are slightly swollen, and have both feet measured, since many people have one foot larger than the other. Avoid narrow toe boxes and very high heels for daily wear. If a shoe rubs when you try it on, it will rub more after a full day of walking, so do not buy it hoping it will stretch enough to fix the problem.

Choose the Right Socks

Socks reduce friction between the foot and the shoe. Look for socks that fit well without bunching, and consider moisture-wicking materials if your feet perspire heavily. Thick, seamless socks are a good choice for people who are prone to soft corns between the toes, because they reduce both friction and moisture.

Use Cushioning and Orthotics

Adhesive pads, toe separators, and corn cushions can protect a vulnerable spot while a corn heals. For recurring calluses caused by pressure patterns, custom or over-the-counter orthotic insoles redistribute weight more evenly across the sole. People with bunions or hammertoes often benefit from toe sleeves or orthotic devices that keep the toes aligned and reduce rubbing.

Keep a Foot Care Routine

Inspect your feet regularly, moisturize daily, and gently smooth developing thick spots before they become painful. Trimming toenails straight across prevents them from pushing neighboring toes into rubbing positions. Small, consistent habits are far more effective than occasional aggressive treatment.

When to See a Podiatrist

Home care is appropriate for mild cases in healthy people, but professional care is the safer choice in several situations. See a podiatrist if a corn or callus is extremely painful, if it keeps returning despite better footwear, if it bleeds or shows signs of infection, or if you are unsure whether the spot is a corn, a wart, or something else. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or nerve damage in the feet should have all corns and calluses treated by a professional, no matter how minor they seem. A podiatrist can safely pare down thickened skin with sterile instruments, identify biomechanical problems, and prescribe orthotics or footwear changes that prevent recurrence.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Are corns and calluses the same thing?

They are related but not identical. Both are thickened skin caused by friction or pressure, but corns are smaller, rounder, and have a dense center that can be painful, while calluses are broader, flatter patches that are usually painless unless they crack. Corns favor the toes, and calluses favor the soles.

2. Will a corn go away on its own?

A corn may soften and shrink if the friction causing it is completely removed, for example by switching to better-fitting shoes. However, most established corns need some gentle treatment, such as soaking and careful filing, before they resolve. If the pressure source remains, the corn will persist or return.

3. How long does it take to remove a callus at home?

With gentle soaking, filing, and moisturizing two to three times per week, a moderate callus usually shows clear improvement within two to four weeks. Very thick, long-standing calluses take longer. Consistency matters more than intensity, and aggressive filing in one session is counterproductive.

4. Why should people with diabetes avoid corn plasters?

Corn plasters contain salicylic acid, which dissolves thickened skin but can also damage surrounding healthy skin. People with diabetes often have reduced sensation and slower healing, so a chemical burn from a corn plaster can turn into an ulcer or infection without them noticing the early warning signs.

5. Can corns and calluses be prevented permanently?

In many cases, yes. Wearing properly fitting shoes, using appropriate socks, addressing pressure patterns with orthotics, and maintaining a simple foot care routine prevent most corns and calluses from forming. Some structural foot issues, such as hammertoes or bunions, may require ongoing management or professional treatment to keep pressure off vulnerable spots.
